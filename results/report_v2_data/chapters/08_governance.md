### Governance & Collective Intelligence

Governance systems today struggle with speed, transparency, and adaptability. These entities share the premise that the core problem is structural: decision-making processes are too slow, opaque, or coercive for 21st-century complexity. Rather than asking "what should we do?", they push toward "how should we decide what to decide, and can we prove it worked?" Each proposes testable, improvable governance infrastructure, treating collective intelligence as something tangible and measurable.



### Competitive Governance Protocol Stack
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 90% | Tech: 81%*

**Significance.** Governance is largely a geographic lottery: individuals are bound by whichever jurisdiction they are born into. This protocol stack would create a governance marketplace where citizens carry portable digital identities, participate in multiple overlapping jurisdictions, and switch between them based on performance. Governance services (legal frameworks, public benefits, dispute resolution) would be selected competitively, with jurisdictions accountable through meaningful exit rights.

**Current state.** The building blocks exist in isolation. [Estonia's e-Residency](https://www.e-resident.gov.ee) has issued digital identities to over 100,000 people since 2014. The [Charter Cities Institute](https://chartercitiesinstitute.org) supports new jurisdictional models, while [Prospera](https://prospera.hn) in Honduras operated under special economic zone rules from 2020 until legal challenges in 2022. [W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/did-core/) finalized Decentralized Identifier and Verifiable Credential standards in 2022, providing the protocol layer for portable credentials. [Gitcoin Passport](https://passport.gitcoin.co) and [Snapshot](https://snapshot.org) enable on-chain governance experiments, though limited to DAOs. Balaji Srinivasan's [The Network State](https://thenetworkstate.com) (2022) articulates the intellectual vision. [Aragon](https://aragon.org) provides decentralized organization tooling. The most promising near-term path involves connecting existing charter city projects through shared identity standards rather than building from scratch.

**Open questions.** Nation-states hold a monopoly on legitimate force and have little incentive to enable frictionless citizen exit. The technical challenges (zero-knowledge credential portability, sybil-resistant identity, gaming-resistant governance metrics) are likely solvable. The deeper problem is bootstrapping: the protocol only becomes valuable when multiple jurisdictions adopt it, and established geopolitical structures actively resist this.



### LexCommons
`Foundational Research` | *d/acc: 90% | Tech: 81%*

**Significance.** Resolving a contract dispute can cost tens of thousands of dollars and take years, placing legal protection out of reach for billions. LexCommons envisions a global legal infrastructure where smart contracts, AI agents, and decentralized governance create an open-source alternative to traditional courts, handling disputes from small claims to international at a fraction of the cost. If realized, it would democratize legal protection and transform law from a closed institutional practice into a participatory global commons.

**Current state.** Early building blocks exist, but no integrated system. [LexDAO](https://www.lexdao.coop/) is the closest incarnation: a decentralized legal engineering guild on Ethereum, active since 2018, developing smart contract tools and legal wrappers for DAOs. [Kleros](https://kleros.io) has run decentralized arbitration on Ethereum since 2018, processing thousands of disputes using crowdsourced jurors incentivized by tokens. AI legal tools like [Harvey AI](https://www.harvey.ai) (founded 2022, backed by Sequoia and OpenAI) handle contract analysis and legal research using large language models. [Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org) has shown open-source licensing works at global scale. These remain separate pieces. No system combines AI legal interpretation, blockchain enforcement, and participatory governance into a unified alternative to state-backed courts. Current smart contracts handle simple "if-then" logic but struggle with ambiguous or context-dependent situations, the core of real legal disputes. Decentralized governance platforms like [Aragon](https://aragon.org) and [Snapshot](https://snapshot.org) provide coordination infrastructure, and AI companies are rapidly improving natural language understanding for legal interpretation. Legal tech startups digitize contracts and dispute resolution but operate within traditional frameworks rather than replacing them.

**Open questions.** The central challenge is legitimacy: legal systems derive authority from state recognition and enforcement power, which no decentralized alternative can replicate without government cooperation. Can code meaningfully replace human judgment for complex disputes? The 2016 DAO hack showed how code-as-law breaks down when outcomes diverge from intent. Cross-jurisdictional harmonization is extraordinarily difficult; the EU spent decades harmonizing commercial law among member states with shared political will. Bar associations actively regulate who can provide legal services, creating barriers to automated alternatives. Without state recognition, LexCommons remains a parallel system with limited authority. The path forward likely involves gradual integration of modular components rather than wholesale replacement.

Note: while smart contract platforms like Ethereum exist, LexCommons represents the not-yet-built layer of AI-native legal agents and cross-jurisdictional open-source governance. It differs from the Competitive Governance Protocol Stack in scope: that entity focuses on jurisdiction-level competition and citizen mobility, while LexCommons targets the legal dispute resolution layer specifically.



### Conflict De-escalation Protocol
`Foundational Research` | *d/acc: 80% | Tech: N/A*

**Significance.** Most conflict resolution systems are adversarial by design: they produce winners and losers, not restored relationships. Courts, arbitration panels, and even human mediators often escalate tensions because they lack tools to dynamically read emotional states, power imbalances, and cultural context in real time. An AI-powered mediation platform that progressively attempts win-win negotiation, then restorative justice, then proportional consequences could reshape dispute resolution from workplace conflicts to international disagreements, democratizing sophisticated strategies currently available only through expensive human experts.

**Current state.** The building blocks exist but not the integration. Online dispute resolution platforms like [Modria](https://www.tylertech.com/Portals/0/OpenContent/Files/4080/Modria-Brochure.pdf) (acquired by Tyler Technologies in 2017) and eBay's system handle over 60 million automated commercial disputes annually, proving algorithmic mediation works for structured conflicts. [Hume AI](https://www.hume.ai) can analyze vocal tone and facial expressions to assess emotional states in real time. Mental health chatbots like [Woebot](https://woebothealth.com) use CBT dialogue strategies similar to de-escalation techniques. The [Consensus Building Institute](https://www.cbi.org) and Harvard's [Program on Negotiation](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/) have developed systematic frameworks that could inform AI design. No system combines emotional intelligence, game-theoretic strategy, and progressive escalation protocols into unified mediation software. Current NLP struggles with the contextual subtlety required; misreading power dynamics or cultural norms risks making things worse. [Kleros](https://kleros.io) uses decentralized arbitration for blockchain disputes but lacks an emotional intelligence layer. [Character.AI](https://character.ai) and [Replika](https://replika.com) demonstrate conversational AI for extended dialogue, while [Polis](https://pol.is) shows how technology can surface common ground in group conflicts. These remain separate capabilities.

**Open questions.** Can AI reliably make normative judgments about fairness across different cultural contexts? The progressive framework requires the system to decide when negotiation has failed and restoration should begin, inherently contested territory. Trust is another barrier: parties in high-stakes conflict are unlikely to accept an AI mediator without demonstrated reliability and institutional backing. Liability is unclear if the system's recommendations escalate rather than resolve disputes. The most pragmatic path starts with low-stakes, structured conflicts (landlord-tenant, workplace disagreements) where training data can be collected and outcomes measured.



### Global Deliberation Coordinator (GDaaS)
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 75% | Tech: 74%*

**Significance.** Global crises like climate change and pandemics require coordinated action, but international institutions move slowly and reflect power politics more than collective wisdom. The Global Deliberation Coordinator proposes AI-mediated platforms enabling statistically representative samples of humanity to deliberate on existential challenges, producing policy recommendations with quantified confidence intervals. This could transform global governance from diplomatic horse-trading into democratic problem-solving at planetary scale.

**Current state.** The concept remains largely hypothetical, though building blocks exist. Taiwan's vTaiwan process has used [Polis](https://pol.is) for AI-augmented deliberation on issues like Uber regulation. The [Collective Intelligence Project](https://www.cip.org) has run "Alignment Assemblies" for public input on AI governance. The OECD documented over 600 citizens' assemblies across 30+ countries by 2024, with organizations like [newDemocracy](https://www.newdemocracy.com.au) and [Sortition Foundation](https://www.sortitionfoundation.org) running these regularly at national scale. Real-time multilingual translation has advanced with [DeepL](https://www.deepl.com) and Meta's [NLLB](https://ai.meta.com/research/no-language-left-behind/). No system currently combines expert validation, representative sampling, cultural sensitivity, and binding authority at global scale. The GDC concept was prototyped at [Foresight Institute](https://foresight.org)'s Existential Hope TAI Institution Design Hackathon in February 2024, winning shared second place. The team included Aviv Ovadya from newDemocracy, Joshua Tan from [Metagov](https://metagov.org/), Evan Miyazono from Atlas Computing, and Bear Haon from Schmidt Futures. They are pursuing "Advanced Market Commitments" from potential users and planning pilot deliberations. Stanford's [Deliberative Democracy Lab](https://deliberation.stanford.edu/) researches best practices, and the UN has experimented with AI-assisted multilingual dialogue tools.

**Open questions.** Three core challenges remain. First, representative sampling: no credible mechanism exists to draw statistically representative panels from 8 billion people, since existing citizens' assemblies rely on national registries that do not exist globally. Second, legitimacy: even well-run deliberations are advisory unless governments commit to implementing outcomes, and major powers show little interest in ceding authority to transnational processes. Third, AI mediation risk: subtle framing effects in how AI systems translate, summarize, or map arguments could systematically bias outcomes in ways participants never notice. Whether meaningful consensus is possible across deep cultural divides, or whether the "service" model would attract genuine engagement, remains open.



#### Additional Entities

**Gevulot (Privacy Infrastructure)**
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 90% | Tech: N/A*
[Gevulot](https://docs.gevulot.com/gevulot-docs/) (now operating as [ZkCloud](https://blog.zkcloud.com/)) proposes granular, dynamic privacy controls letting individuals specify exactly what information about themselves can be captured and who can access it, moment by moment. Today's privacy tools force an all-or-nothing choice: surrender data completely or block access entirely. By making privacy granular and dynamic, Gevulot aims to rebuild trust in data sharing while preserving individual autonomy. Gevulot is an early-stage project, not yet deployed; what is described here is the full vision, most of which remains to be built.

**Habermas Machines**
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 75% | Tech: 60%*
[Habermas Machines](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adq2852) are AI systems that help diverse groups find common ground by processing participants' input into clearer collective statements, cutting through communication breakdowns and power dynamics that usually derail group decisions. Named after philosopher Jurgen Habermas and [developed by Google DeepMind](https://github.com/google-deepmind/habermas_machine), they aim to salvage democratic deliberation when polarization, information overload, and bad-faith debate make consensus difficult. If effective, they could transform everything from corporate board meetings to citizen assemblies, though they raise questions about whether algorithms should mediate human understanding. Note: Habermas Machines focus on small-group consensus generation, while GDaaS targets planetary-scale representative deliberation.
