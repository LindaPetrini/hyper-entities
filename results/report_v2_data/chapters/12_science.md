### Science & Discovery

What links these entities is the drive to automate, accelerate, or restructure how scientific knowledge is created, validated, and shared. Some are practical infrastructure: publishing systems serving AI researchers, or protein design platforms targeting real-world problems. Others are more speculative, like AI discovering patterns without human-legible theory. Together, they reveal a field grappling with whether tomorrow's science will simply be faster, or categorically different: machine-readable, massively collaborative, and potentially illegible to human scientists.



### Automated Scientific Publishing Ecosystem for Machine Consumers
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 90% | Tech: 86%*

**Significance.** Scientific knowledge moves at human speed. Papers take months to years between completion and publication, creating a bottleneck as research complexity explodes. Current publishing infrastructure treats AI as an afterthought, locking knowledge in PDFs behind paywalls while researchers struggle with exponential literature growth. A publishing system designed for machine consumers could enable real-time knowledge synthesis across disciplines, letting AI verify findings, flag contradictions, and compress discovery cycles from years to days.

**Current state.** The building blocks exist but remain fragmented. [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org) from the [Allen Institute for AI](https://allenai.org) has created structured, machine-readable representations of 200+ million papers. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org) and [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org) have normalized instant preprint sharing. The [FAIR data principles](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/), now mandated by major funders, push datasets toward machine readability, and [OpenAlex](https://openalex.org) provides free, structured metadata for the entire scholarly record. AI-native tools like [Elicit](https://elicit.com) and [Scite.ai](https://scite.ai) already parse and synthesize literature at scale. [ResearchHub](https://www.researchhub.com) and [DeSci Labs](https://desci.com) experiment with blockchain-verified research artifacts and tokenized peer review. The [Center for Open Science](https://www.cos.io) champions machine-readable preregistration and open data through the [Open Science Framework](https://osf.io). [Perplexity AI](https://www.perplexity.ai) cites sources in real-time answer generation, creating demand for better-structured scientific metadata. The European Commission's [European Open Science Cloud](https://eosc.eu) aims to make all publicly funded research machine-accessible by 2030, though implementation remains patchy. The complete ecosystem is missing: no consensus metadata standards for machine-verifiable claims, no trusted automated peer review, and no infrastructure for AI to directly contribute findings.

**Open questions.** Can automated systems develop the domain expertise and contextual judgment to replace human peer review, or will they remain auxiliary tools? The deeper barrier is institutional: Elsevier and Springer Nature control most published science and profit from human-oriented paywalls, giving them little incentive to restructure. Creating consensus around metadata standards historically takes decades. Most uncertain is whether machine-generated contributions can achieve academic legitimacy: if an AI discovers a pattern across 10,000 papers, who authors it, who verifies it, and how do institutions decide it counts for tenure or funding?



### Universal AI Learning UnCommons (UALU)
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 85% | Tech: 63%*

**Significance.** Today's AI education landscape is dominated by proprietary platforms from tech giants and elite universities, creating fragmented experiences that exclude indigenous knowledge systems, non-Western pedagogies, and marginalized communities. UALU envisions a federated network where AI educational tools are co-created and governed by diverse councils (elders, learners, technologists, ethicists) rather than corporate incentives. How AI literacy is taught shapes who builds AI, whose values get embedded, and whether AI amplifies or challenges knowledge monocultures.

**Current state.** Pieces exist but the whole does not. [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) partnered with OpenAI in 2023 to launch Khanmigo, demonstrating AI tutoring at scale. [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co) and [fast.ai](https://www.fast.ai) have built large open-source learning communities. [P2PU](https://www.p2pu.org) and Wikipedia show how volunteer-driven knowledge commons operate with distributed governance. The [Global Indigenous Data Alliance](https://www.gida-global.org) (established 2019) has developed frameworks for culturally appropriate technology governance. [OpenMined](https://openmined.org) has built federated learning infrastructure keeping data decentralized. The concept draws on governance experiments in both tech ([Aragon](https://aragon.org), [Snapshot](https://snapshot.org) for DAO coordination) and education (P2PU's peer learning circles, [Open edX](https://openedx.org)'s institutional adopters), with [Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org) providing legal infrastructure. No one has combined open educational platforms, AI tutoring, multi-stakeholder governance, and indigenous knowledge integration into a single functioning system. Most work remains theoretical or in isolated pilots.

**Open questions.** The central paradox: coordinating between AI researchers, indigenous elders, ethicists, and diverse learners requires bridging very different epistemological frameworks and technological literacy levels, a governance challenge no platform has solved at scale. How can quality be ensured across hundreds of community contexts without centralized control? Who pays for ongoing infrastructure when community-governed commons lack corporate revenue and grant funding is short-term? Can volunteer capacity handle the continuous auditing needed for sound, culturally appropriate, technically accurate content? Can a federated system resist eventual capture by well-resourced actors who can afford sustained participation while grassroots communities cannot?



#### Additional Entities

**Decentralized Scientific Collaboration Infrastructure**
`Scaling Challenges` | *d/acc: 90% | Tech: 76%*
Today's scientific system forces researchers to compete for scarce positions and publication slots, creating perverse incentives that prioritize individual credit over collaboration and discourage open sharing. Decentralized infrastructure would replace this with federated networks where researchers coordinate across institutions, AI handles routine tasks and matches complementary expertise, and transparent attribution rewards all contributions (data collection, replication), not just first authorship. Most hard problems (climate, pandemics) require integrating insights across disciplines, something publish-or-perish actively prevents. Platforms like [Molecule](https://molecule.xyz) and the broader [DeSci movement](https://ethereum.org/en/desci/) are building early infrastructure using blockchain-based IP tools and tokenized incentives. Note: while the Automated Scientific Publishing Ecosystem (above) focuses on dissemination and machine consumption, this entity addresses how researchers coordinate during the research process itself.

**Atheoretical Science AI**
`Foundational Research` | *d/acc: 65% | Tech: N/A*
Atheoretical Science AI discovers scientific patterns in massive datasets without requiring prior theories, letting algorithms explore data like a child learning language before learning grammar. This matters because major breakthroughs often come from noticing what existing theories cannot explain, but human scientists can only look where their theories direct them, potentially missing entire classes of phenomena. If successful, it could accelerate discovery in fields drowning in data but starved for interpretable insights. Critics worry it may generate correlations without causation; recent work on combining [data-driven AI with scientific reasoning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-37236-y) suggests hybrid approaches may prove more fruitful.

**Protein Design for Global Challenges**
`Near Deployment` | *d/acc: 65% | Tech: 76%*
Proteins are molecular machines programmable to do specific jobs: breaking down plastics, capturing carbon, or delivering drugs precisely where needed. While naturally occurring proteins have long been used (enzymes in laundry detergent), AI breakthroughs like [AlphaFold](https://deepmind.google/science/alphafold/) and tools from the [Institute for Protein Design](https://www.ipd.uw.edu/) now allow engineering novel proteins from scratch. Many of the hardest global challenges, from climate change to antibiotic resistance, require molecular-scale solutions that traditional materials cannot achieve. David Baker's 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for computational protein design underscores how rapidly this field has matured.

**Origin of Life Experimental Platform**
`Early Demonstrations` | *d/acc: 50% | Tech: 66%*
Scientists do not know how life began, and current experiments test only a tiny fraction of possible chemical pathways one at a time. An Origin of Life Experimental Platform would use robots and AI to search millions of molecular combinations systematically, treating life's emergence as a solvable physics problem rather than a historical mystery. [Lee Cronin's lab](https://www.chem.gla.ac.uk/cronin/) at Glasgow has pioneered the "[chemputer](https://www.chem.gla.ac.uk/cronin/)" concept: a programmable, automated chemistry system for exploring prebiotic chemical space at scale. Discovering how chemistry spontaneously organizes into biology could unlock principles for engineering new life forms, with implications for synthetic biology, planetary science, and the search for extraterrestrial life.
